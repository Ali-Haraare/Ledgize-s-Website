You check your October numbers and Amazon looks slow. Then November looks like a record month. Nothing changed in the business. The sales just landed in the wrong month, because of how Amazon pays you.

Amazon pays in settlements, not by calendar month

Amazon doesn't send you each sale as it happens. It collects sales, fees, refunds and other charges over a settlement period, usually about two weeks, then pays out what's left. Those periods run on their own schedule, not on calendar months, so one settlement often covers the last days of one month and the first days of the next.

There's a second delay too. For many orders, Amazon holds the money until after the order is delivered. A sale made on October 30 can be paid out in a settlement that closes in November.

An example

Example figures

One Amazon settlement that crosses month end:

Sales, October 25 to 31$6,000
Sales, November 1 to 7$8,000
Amazon fees and refunds for the period−$4,200
Deposit to your bank, in November$9,800

These figures are for illustration only.

If the whole settlement is recorded when the money arrives, all $14,000 of sales goes into November. October's Amazon sales look $6,000 lower than they were, and November's look $6,000 higher. The fees move with them, so October's fee rate and margin are wrong too.

Why it matters

  • Month-to-month comparisons stop meaning anything. A "great November" can be partly October's sales. A "bad October" can be a timing gap, not a real slowdown.
  • Channel decisions get made on the wrong numbers. If Amazon looks weak one month and strong the next, it's hard to judge whether an ad campaign or a price change worked.
  • Year end is affected. Sales from late December can end up in January, in a different tax year. Your CPA will want them in the right year.

What fixed looks like

  1. Each settlement is split by the dates the sales actually happened, so October's sales and fees are recorded in October and November's in November.
  2. Money Amazon owes you but hasn't paid yet sits in a clearing account, not in your bank balance and not in a future month's sales. When the deposit arrives, it clears that account.
  3. Money Amazon holds back as a reserve is tracked separately, so you can see what's yours but not yet available.

This isn't practical to do by hand every two weeks. A settlement connector does it. I use Link My Books, including its Amazon deferred transactions add-on, which records Amazon sales in the month they were made even when Amazon pays them out later.

A quick check you can do today

In Seller Central, run Amazon's date range report for last month (it's under Payments, in Reports). Compare the sales total with your Amazon sales for the same month in your books. If they're far apart, and the gap moves from month to month, your books are likely recording settlements when they're paid rather than when the sales happened.
