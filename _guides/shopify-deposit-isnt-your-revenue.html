Every few days, Shopify Payments sends money to your bank. It's tempting to treat each deposit as sales: the money came in, so that's what you sold. But a payout isn't one thing. It's several things combined into a single number, and recording it as revenue is one of the most common reasons an e-commerce profit and loss looks healthier than the business really is.

What's inside a Shopify payout

A single payout usually combines:

  • Product sales: what customers paid for your products, after discounts.
  • Shipping charged to customers.
  • Sales tax you collected: money you're holding for the states you sold into. It isn't income.
  • Refunds, taken back out of the payout.
  • Processing fees: what Shopify Payments charges for each card payment.
  • Sometimes also chargebacks and their fees, Shopify Capital repayments, or adjustments.

Shopify adds all of this up and sends you what's left. The deposit is the end of the calculation, not the start.

An example

Example figures

One week of orders on a Shopify store:

Product sales$10,000
Shipping charged to customers$600
Sales tax collected$700
Refunds (product only)−$400
Processing fees−$330
Payout to your bank$10,570

These figures are for illustration only.

What goes wrong when the deposit is booked as revenue

If the $10,570 goes into your books as one line of sales:

  • Revenue is wrong. Your real revenue for the week is $10,200: product sales and shipping, less refunds. The deposit says $10,570.
  • Your fees disappear. The $330 never shows up as a cost, so you can't see what card processing really costs you.
  • Your refunds disappear. A rising refund rate is one of the earliest warning signs in an e-commerce business, and it's hidden inside a smaller deposit.
  • Sales tax looks like income. The $700 belongs to the states you collected it for. Counted as revenue, it inflates your profit, and it's easy to spend before it's due.

Example figures, side by side

Deposit booked as revenuePayout split into its parts
Revenue$10,570$10,200
Processing fees$0$330
Sales tax owed to the statesNot recorded$700
Profit before product and other costs$10,570$9,870

The difference is $700, exactly the sales tax. In this example, booking the deposit makes the week look $700 more profitable than it was, and that's money you'll have to pay out. Across a year of payouts, on Shopify, Amazon and eBay, the gap adds up.

What fixed looks like

In books that are set up for e-commerce:

  1. Each payout is recorded as a summary of its parts, with sales, shipping, refunds, fees and sales tax each on their own line.
  2. The payout goes through a clearing account first, then to the bank, so the deposit matches to the cent. If the clearing account doesn't come back to zero, something is missing or posted twice.
  3. Sales tax sits on the balance sheet as money you owe, not in revenue.
  4. Fees and refunds have their own lines in your profit and loss, so you can watch them month to month.

None of this has to be done by hand. A settlement connector reads each Shopify payout and posts it to QuickBooks Online already split into its parts. I use Link My Books for this, and it handles Amazon and eBay payouts the same way.

A quick check you can do today

Open one recent payout in your Shopify admin and look at the transactions inside it. Then find the same deposit in your books. If the books show one line of income for the full deposit amount, your revenue, fees, refunds and sales tax are all blended together, and your profit is likely overstated by at least the sales tax you collected.

Sales tax rules depend on the states you sell into, and marketplaces collect it for you in some cases. If you're not sure how yours should be handled, it's worth confirming with your CPA.
